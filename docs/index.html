Why night lights matter

Artificial light at night disrupts nocturnal insects, birds and other wildlife, affects human sleep and health, wastes energy and hides the stars. In Vietnam it does not only come from cities: in Binh Thuan, farmers keep millions of bulbs on in dragon fruit fields at night so the plants flower out of season, which makes farmland glow as brightly as a town. Night lights are a direct footprint of urban growth, electrification and farming practices, so their trends show where and how fast human activity is changing.

Beyond the satellite: what explains the Binh Thuan drop?

Satellite data show what changed. To test why, we compared our results with independent reports. These sources make one explanation consistent with the data; they do not prove it.

Our reading: brightness fell more than farmland area (−35% vs −17%), which fits a mix of fewer fields, less off-season lighting while prices were low, and more efficient bulbs. In the Ham Thuan Bac belt, the only area of central and southern Vietnam where brightness fell significantly, the lit area stayed flat (77% to 84%) while brightness fell from 105 to 61 nW/cm²/sr: fields stayed lit but got dimmer, which fits the bulb change better than abandoned fields. Separating these causes needs field data (lighting hours, bulb types per farm).

Detective glossary

Radiance (nW/cm²/sr)
How much light the satellite measures coming up from the ground at night, per area and viewing angle. Higher = brighter.
Black Marble (VNP46A4)
NASA's annual night-light composite from the VIIRS instrument on Suomi-NPP, corrected for moonlight, clouds, atmosphere and snow.
Trend
A consistent rise or fall over time, not just a one-year jump.
Theil–Sen slope
The median of the slopes between every pair of years. It tells you "how much per year" and is not fooled by a single odd year.
Mann–Kendall test
A test that counts how often later years are higher than earlier years. It does not assume the data are normally distributed.
p-value
The chance of seeing a trend this strong if there were really no trend. Below 0.05 we call the trend statistically significant (95% confidence).
Not significant?
Still interesting! The change may be real but too small or too noisy for the period — a longer record may confirm it.

Data, method & limitations

Data: NASA Black Marble VNP46A4 Collection 2 (annual, 15 arc-second ≈ 500 m), layer AllAngle_Composite_Snow_Free, 2012–2025, from NASA LAADS DAAC. Method: clip each region, average valid pixels per year, Mann–Kendall test (α = 0.05) and Theil–Sen slope for the regional series and for every pixel. The period selector re-runs the test in your browser.

Limitations: Trends show what changed, not why. Causes need independent data, such as the reports listed above. VIIRS Day/Night Band is not sensitive to blue light below ~500 nm, so switching to white LEDs can look like dimming even if sky brightness increases. The satellite sees upward light at ~01:30 local time, not the sky glow people experience. Annual composites hide seasonal and event-level changes (e.g. typhoon blackouts). We analyse linear (monotonic) trends only.
